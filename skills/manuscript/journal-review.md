# Journal Review Response

## Zen

This is the TLDR. Everything else in this file expands on these points.

- **Every point gets a response.** No exceptions. If the reviewer wrote it, you
  address it.
- **The reviewer is always right.** Even when they are wrong. Your job is not to
  win an argument; your job is to get the paper accepted.
- **Be polite, be diligent, be comprehensive — but never bloated.** A thick
  response letter makes reviewers think "this author works hard, I should let
  them pass." A rambling one makes them think "this author has no clue."
- **Read the tone behind the words.** A soft reviewer may be raising a fatal
  flaw. A harsh reviewer may be raising a routine fix.

## The Review Landscape

When you submit a manuscript, the editor first decides whether it meets basic
requirements. If so, they typically send it to three reviewers.

### The Myth of Anonymity

Peer review is supposed to be anonymous, but in practice it rarely stays that
way forever. Experienced scholars can often tell where a manuscript is from —
the literature cited, the method used, the data source. Conversely, authors
sometimes recognize who wrote a review by the specific concerns raised or the
way they are phrased. This creates a curious social dynamic: the small world of
academia means you may review someone's paper today, and they may review yours
tomorrow. This is worth keeping in mind, but it is not a principle to govern
your behavior; simply a fact of the landscape.

### What Reviewers Actually Want

Reviewers are rarely volunteers in the romantic sense. They are usually
"voluntold" — asked by editors because they are expected to serve. Reviewing
brings little direct credit: it does not advance your tenure case, it does not
appear on your CV, and it takes time away from your own work. Scholars do it
because refusing too often signals that you are not contributing to the field,
which departments, editors, and colleagues notice.

In special issues or tight editorial networks, reviewing can be a straight
exchange: the editor does you a favor by considering your paper, and you repay
that favor by reviewing someone else's. The reviewer may have no intrinsic
interest in your work at all.

Many faculty further delegate reviews to graduate students or, increasingly, to
AI tools. The goal is often not to improve your paper but to complete the task
with plausible effort. Some reviewers may simply dislike you, your method, or
your institution. None of this changes how you respond. Whether they are
engaged, indifferent, or hostile, your tone remains the same: submissive,
grateful, and polite.

## Reading Reviews

### The Structure of a Review

Reviews typically open with a positive summary: "This is an interesting study
that addresses an important question..." Do not relax. What matters is what
comes after the "but":

> "...but there are several concerns that need to be addressed before this
> manuscript is suitable for publication."

The opening praise is social lubrication. The real verdict is in the critique
that follows.

### Tone vs. Severity

Learn to read the gap between tone and actual severity:

- **Soft tone, hard problem.** A reviewer may praise your work extensively and
  phrase their concerns gently — but the underlying issue may require you to
  restructure your method, collect new data, or rethink your central claim.
  These are the reviews that quietly kill papers.

- **Harsh tone, easy problem.** A reviewer may write in an aggressive or
  dismissive voice, raising what appear to be devastating objections — but on
  closer inspection, the concerns are routine, addressable with a paragraph or a
  supplementary analysis. Sometimes this bluster is performative: the reviewer
  wants to signal that they are thorough and tough, even when the actual work
  required is minor.

Do not confuse volume with severity. A short, calm sentence may contain a
paper-killer. A long, angry paragraph may contain nothing you cannot fix in an
afternoon.

### The Paper-Killer

Some reviews ask for something you fundamentally cannot deliver: a different
methodology, new data collection, a redesign of your entire study. These are not
requests — they are rejections dressed as feedback. You must recognize them for
what they are. The appropriate response is not to attempt the impossible; it is
to address what you can, explain the constraints politely, and let the editor
weigh the situation.

## The Response Letter

The response letter is your primary deliverable. The updated manuscript matters,
but the letter is what the editor and reviewers read first. It sets the tone for
how your revision is evaluated.

### Cover Letter to the Editor

Open with a brief, polite summary:

```markdown
Dear Dr. [Editor Name],

Thank you for the opportunity to revise and resubmit our manuscript, "[Title],"
to [Journal Name]. We appreciate the time and effort that you and the reviewers
have dedicated to evaluating our work. We have carefully considered all comments
and have revised the manuscript accordingly. Below we provide a point-by-point
response to the reviewers' comments.

We hope that the revised manuscript now meets the standards of [Journal Name].

Sincerely, [Corresponding Author]
```

Keep it short. One paragraph is enough.

### Point-by-Point Response

Organize by reviewer. For each reviewer, quote their points verbatim, then
respond directly underneath.

```markdown
## Response to Reviewer 1

We thank the reviewer for the thorough and constructive review. The following
are our responses.

- _[Paste the reviewer's exact text here, verbatim.]_

  [Your response. Be specific. Reference where changes appear: "We have added a
  discussion of this limitation in Section 3.2 (p. 12, l. 15–18)." If the change
  is substantial, quote the new text. If you cannot comply, explain why politely
  and offer what you can.]

- _[Another point, quoted verbatim.]_

  [Response...]
```

### Key Rules

- **Quote verbatim.** Do not paraphrase. The reviewer should recognize their own
  words. This shows you engaged with their exact concern.
- **Answer every point.** Even if a comment is about a typo, acknowledge it.
  Even if you disagree, address it. Never skip a point.
- **Reference locations.** Always tell the reviewer where to look: section,
  page, line, figure, or appendix. Do not make them hunt.
- **Show, don't just tell.** If a change is substantial, quote the revised text
  in the response. If it is minor, a location reference suffices.

## Philosophy

### The Response Letter as Social Signal

The response letter conveys two things simultaneously: information (what you
changed) and attitude (how seriously you took the review). A letter that is both
comprehensive and concise signals that you are diligent, cooperative, and
professional. It makes the reviewer think: "They wrote so carefully, it would be
unfair to reject them."

### Do Not Fight the Reviewer

Reviewers have a say over whether your paper lives or dies. That means
everything. Even if a reviewer is factually wrong, even if their criticism is
unreasonable, you must address it with courtesy and care. If they misread your
method, do not write "The reviewer has misunderstood..." Instead: "We thank the
reviewer for raising this point. To clarify, our method operates as follows..."

If a request is impossible, do not say "This cannot be done." Instead: "We
appreciate this suggestion. The requested analysis is not feasible with our
existing data because [reason]. To address the underlying concern, we have
instead [alternative]."

### The Goal Is Acceptance, Not Being Right

You are not writing a rebuttal in a debate tournament. You are writing to
persuade the editor and reviewers that your paper deserves to be published. If
being right jeopardizes acceptance, you are doing it wrong. The best response to
a bad review is a good revision.

>
