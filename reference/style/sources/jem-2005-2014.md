# JEM Prose Conventions: A Style Report for AI Writers

Corpus: seven Journal of Educational Measurement articles (2005-2014), files under `repertoire/jem/md/`. Short keys used below:

- **A** = `10.1111:j.1745-3984.2005.00007.md` (mixture IRT for DIF; two-study empirical)
- **B** = `10.1111:j.1745-3984.2006.00009.x.md` (constant difference model for selection bias; conceptual/mathematical)
- **C** = `10.1111:j.1745-3984.2009.00083.x.md` (CDR vs. logistic regression DIF detection; simulation)
- **D** = `10.1111:j.1745-3984.2012.00176.x.md` (five calibration procedures under speededness; simulation + empirical)
- **E** = `10.1111:jedm.12027.md` (piecewise linear postsmoothing in equating; simulation)
- **F** = `10.1111:jedm.12048.md` (nonparametric classification accuracy/consistency; simulation + empirical)
- **G** = `10.1111:jedm.12050.md` (book review; included for register contrast)

## 1. Document Architecture

Unstructured single-paragraph abstracts of 150-250 words. No keywords, no structured headings. The abstract moves: problem -> gap -> method (one sentence: "A simulation study was used to assess...") -> results in qualitative comparative terms -> one applied payoff. Numbers in abstracts are rare; findings are stated as orderings ("the power of CDR was consistently higher than that of LR across all forms of DIF", C).

Section sequence is IMRaD but with JEM-specific furniture:

- **Introduction is untitled.** After the abstract, body prose begins immediately with no "Introduction" heading (all of A, B, C, D, E, F).
- A **methods-review section** often sits between intro and Method, titled by content, not "Literature Review": "## Description of Relevant DIF Detection Procedures" (C), "## Item Calibration Procedures" (D), "## The Nonparametric Approach to Estimate CA and CC" (F), "## Thorndike's Constant Ratio Model" / "## Fixed-n vs. n-Free Selection" (B). Equations are introduced here, not in the intro.
- Method section: "## Method" (C, E) or "### Methods" as a run-in bold under a study heading (A) or "### Methods" under "## Simulation Study of CA and CC Estimators" (F). D names the whole block "## Simulation Study" with "### Simulation Design" and "### Estimation and Evaluative Measures".
- Subsection titling is content-driven and sparse: "### Sample Size", "### Ability Distribution Differences" (C); "### Population Distributions and Equating Functions", "#### Raw equipercentile equating", "#### Postsmoothing methods", "#### Presmoothing methods" (E). Deeper levels go to #### at most.
- Bold run-in paragraph headers substitute for formal subsections in methods: "**Data. **", "**DIF detection. **", "**Estimation of the latent group membership. **" (A); "**_MH procedure_**", "**_BD procedure_**", "**_LR procedure_**" (C).
- A **named empirical section** follows the simulation: "## An Empirical Illustration" (D), "## Empirical Data Example" (F).
- Closing heading is "## Discussion" (C, E, F), "## Conclusion" (A), "## Conclusion and Discussion" (D), "## Concluding Remarks" (B). Multi-study papers use "## Study 1: Gender DIF in a University Mathematics Placement Test" and "## Study 2: ..." (A), each with internal "### Methods" / "### Results" and a bold run-in "**Discussion of results of Study 1. **".
- Back matter: "# Notes" (footnotes), "## Authors" (bios in a fixed formula: "X is Associate Professor, Department of ..., University ...; email. His/Her primary research interests include ..."), "## References", optional "# Appendix" with software code (A).

## 2. Rhetorical Moves

**Opening strategies.** Three openings dominate: (a) a plain-language problem any practitioner recognizes -- "In any assessment of individuals, test developers want to be sure that the scores are free of bias so that they are valid" (C); "There are many reasons why examinees might not answer all items on a test" (D); (b) a construct definition -- "Classification accuracy (CA) is the rate at which observed scores from a test classify examinees into the correct category" (F); (c) a historical/practice anchor -- "Linear functions have a long history of applications in test equating" (E). No paper opens with a citation, a statistic, or a provocative claim.

**Gap statements** are short, adversative, and named against specific predecessors: "Penfield's (2003a) study did not, however, directly compare LR and CDR under conditions in which the DIF was both uniform and nonuniform, and thus it is still unknown whether CDR provides a relative advantage over LR for both forms of DIF" (C); "Although several approaches have been proposed to account for test speededness, each using a real data set, no further work has been reported comparing these various approaches" (D); "Once a differential item functioning (DIF) item has been identified, little is known about the examinees for whom the item functions differentially" (A); "Although a variety of statistical procedures have been developed for detecting DIF, no single method is effective for detecting both uniform and nonuniform DIF" (C).

**Purpose statements** come late in the intro (final or penultimate paragraph) and are explicit: "Therefore, the purpose of this study is to investigate, through simulations, the relative performance of five item calibration procedures for speeded item responses ... under various testing conditions" (D); "The major purpose of this study was to comparatively evaluate the accuracy of APLAs and cubic spline postsmoothing methods" (E, with "A secondary question of this study was how APLAs and cubic spline methods compare with 'presmoothing equating' methods"); "This study seeks to compare LR and CDR under conditions of both uniform and nonuniform DIF to provide insight concerning which method is preferred over all forms of DIF observed in applied testing situations" (C). Research questions are stated as prose, never as numbered "RQ1:" lists.

**Roadmap paragraph** is optional; when present it is pedestrian: "The remaining sections of the article are laid out as follows. In the following section, the description of the five different item calibration procedures is provided" (D).

**Limitations** are framed as a numbered/ordered inventory introduced by a stock pivot: "Although the results of this study indicate that CDR was the single most effective method for assessing both uniform and nonuniform DIF, there are several limitations of these results that deserve recognition. First, ... Second, ... Finally, ..." (C); "With that said, the study is limited in several aspects. First, ... Second, ... Third, ..." (F); "There are several limitations to the current study" (D). Limitations almost always convert into future-research imperatives: "more research is needed in this area" (C); "It therefore is left for a future study to compare these newly developed models with the models presented in this study" (D).

**Implications for practice** are addressed to "practitioners" / "test practitioners" explicitly (5 hits for *practitioner*): "the results from this simulation study would appear to have several implications for how practitioners select and understand item calibration procedures when working with incomplete data due to test speededness" (D); recommendations are direct second-order imperatives: "we recommend using the nonparametric approach as a safer, if not better, method to estimate CA and CC" (F); "the omitted scoring should be preferred for item calibration if one has no choice but to use the 2PL model on heavily speeded data" (D).

## 3. Paragraph Patterns

Paragraphs are long (6-12 sentences) and deductive. The topic sentence states the claim or the organizational fact; the body elaborates, defines, or cites; the last sentence delivers the consequence. Method paragraphs open with procedural topic sentences in passive past ("Responses to an artificial test were generated according to a three-parameter logistic model", C) and proceed in chronological order of what was done. Results paragraphs open with a table pointer plus the headline pattern ("Table 4 presents the rejection rates for MH, BD, CDR, and LR ... as a function of sample size and equality of the reference and focal group ability distributions", C), then move from the most important pattern to exceptions. Discussion paragraphs open by restating one finding in plain words, then interpret. Transitions between paragraphs are lexical and minimal: "However,", "In addition,", "In contrast,", "For example,", "As expected,", "Finally,". Enumeration words "First, ... Second, ... Third, ... Finally, ..." organize both discussion inventories and within-paragraph argument (7/5/3/14 hits respectively).

## 4. Sentence-Level Grammar

**Person and voice by section.** Introductions mix third-person expository present with occasional "we" for moves ("We first analyze item response patterns for gender DIF and then illustrate...", A; "We submit that the failure of this approach ... is systemic", B). Methods are overwhelmingly passive past: "Two samples of 1,000 examinees ... were randomly drawn" (A); "Responses were generated for the 60-item test using the IRT-Lab software package" (C); "Fifty replications were simulated for each condition" (D). Across the corpus, "was used" (18), "were used" (17), "was conducted" (3), "were generated" (5), "were drawn" (6) vs. "we" total only 41 hits, concentrated in A, B, and F. Results use present-tense attribution to tables ("Table 5 displays the rejection rate", C) and past tense for what happened ("the power of all procedures was higher than that in Item 2", C). Discussion returns to present tense for claims ("the nonparametric approach is often closer to the empirical baseline", F).

**Tense rule of thumb:** literature and settled knowledge = present ("The MH chi-square yields the uniformly most powerful unbiased test", C); study conduct = past; findings as durable facts = past in Results, present when generalized in Discussion; tables/figures = present ("shows", "presents", "displays").

**Hedging inventory (verbatim):** "may be" (25), "would appear to" ("the results of this simulation study would appear to have several implications", D), "appears to be" ("CDR appears to be an attractive alternative", C), "seem to" / "seems to" (4), "might be" (6), "could be" (6), "would be" (10), "may not", "tend to" ("missing percentages tended to be higher in the speeded class", D), "This result suggests that CDR may be the single most effective method" (C), "probably" ("is probably misleading", A), "approximately", "relatively" (very frequent: "relatively high power", "relatively low", "relatively similar results"), "often" ("is often closer to the empirical baseline", F), "generally" ("the estimates are generally very good", F). Claims are hedged at the verb, rarely with modal adverb stacks.

**Simulation conditions** are described as fully crossed factors with exact levels named: "Crossing the two levels each of sample size and ability distribution equality yielded four conditions. Each condition was replicated 200 times" (C); "the design of the simulation study includes 16 conditions resulting from four fully crossed factors" (D). Justification of each level is mandatory and cites precedent: "These distributions were chosen to resemble values observed in previous simulation studies" (D); "The test length of 60 items was chosen to provide a realistic number of items ... and is similar to test lengths used in previous simulation studies of DIF" (C).

## 5. Lexicon

Domain nouns by raw count across the seven files: *item* 501, *DIF* 518, *test* 332, *parameter* 246, *bias* 200, *examinee* 216, *condition* 175, *estimate* 147, *equating* 127, *sample size* 74, *power* 62, *performance* 51, *ability distribution* 47, *accuracy* 35, *advantage* 33, *Type I error* 29, *cut score* 17, *replication* 4.

Characteristic verbs: *examine* family (239, the workhorse), *estimate* (147), *indicate* (43), *evaluate* (43), *assess* (38), *suggest* (36), *investigate* (33), *illustrate* (20), *propose* (19), *develop* (18), *perform* (16, as in "the hybrid model showed less accuracy", "performed similarly"), *demonstrate* (8). Reporting verb of choice is *indicate/show/suggest*, never *prove*. Comparatives are *higher/lower than*, *similar to*, *comparable to*, *outperform* (2).

Stock collocations: "the purpose of this study" (2), "It should be noted that" (6), "as expected" (5), "consistent with" (13), "with respect to" (18), "in terms of" (23), "compared to" (13), "rather than" (10), "in practice" (12), "note that" (9), "as shown in Table" (4), "nominal level" (10), "real data" (7), "e.g.," (24), "i.e.," (80; heavy), "cf." (0; unused). Latinate connector stack: "however" (37), "although" (18), "in particular" (5), "for example" (13), "in general" (4), "namely" (3), "to summarize" (3), "in summary" (2).

## 6. Math in Prose

Equations are numbered, displayed, and always preceded and followed by verbal gloss. The introduction formula is a "let"-statement or a naming sentence: "To describe MH, BD, and LR, let us consider a test yielding possible score levels j = 0, 1, ... , J" (C); "Then the probability of a correct response to the item is modeled as:" (A); "This method is developed as follows" (C); "The hybrid model is described as follows:" (D). After each equation, every symbol is defined in a "where"-sentence enumerating terms in order ("where R is the number of replications", D). Interpretation follows in plain English: "If there is no DIF, only beta0 and beta1 should be nonzero. The extent to which beta2 differs from zero provides evidence of uniform DIF" (C). Derivations are walked stepwise with connective phrases: "Expanding Equation 4 and setting the result equal to zero yields ... which is quadratic with respect to Aj" (C). The applied audience gets every equation twice: once symbolic, once verbal. Statistical shorthand appears inline with italicized letters: "(M = .98, SD = 1.06)", "(p < .01)", "rho = .13" (A).

## 7. Reporting Results

Results are narrated table-by-table, one paragraph per display, in the formula: display pointer -> headline pattern -> condition-level detail with numbers -> exception. Reference formulas: "Table 4 presents the rejection rates for MH, BD, CDR, and LR" (C); "Tables 3 and 4 show bias values for slope and intercept parameters across all simulation conditions" (D); "Table 7 shows RMSDs between each pair of the five models" (D); "Results summarized in Table 2 show that the M3plM solution yielded mixing proportions of approximately 57% in Class 1" (A); "As shown in Table 1, the design of the simulation study includes 16 conditions" (D). Figures: "Figures ... plot the biases of the spline and APLA results" (E). Numbers are compared as parenthetical pairs: "(.75 for the CDR vs. .42 for LR-C)" (C); "ranged from 95% to 99%" (D). Proportions reported to 2-3 decimals with no leading zero (".05", ".92", "−.05"). Summaries close the Results with "To summarize, the results of the simulation study indicated that..." (C). Interpretive load is carried by "implying" / "indicating" clauses: "implying that both models performed well in terms of classifying examinees into the specified groups regardless of various simulation conditions" (D).

## 8. Citation Practices

Both narrative and parenthetical citations, with parenthetical clusters for scene-setting ("(e.g., Lord, 1980; Thissen, Steinberg, & Wainer, 1988, 1993)", A) and narrative for load-bearing predecessors ("Swaminathan and Rogers (1990) applied the LR model to the detection of both uniform and nonuniform DIF", C; "Penfield (2003a) found that BD displayed a high power...", C). Long enumeration clusters are used to establish a method's popularity: "(California Department of Education, , p. 367; Goffreda & DiPerna, ; ... Wheadon & Stockford, )" (F). "e.g.," introduces citation clusters (24 hits); "i.e.," is the dominant clarification device (80); "cf." is absent. Politeness toward prior work: criticism is attributed, softened, and paired with acknowledgment -- "Penfield's (2003a) study did not, however, directly compare LR and CDR" (C); "The weaknesses found in LR, MH, and BD suggest the need for a computationally simple method" (C); praise is functional ("This finding is not surprising because MH was designed to detect only uniform DIF", C). Agreement with predecessors is a required Discussion move: "The results of this study were consistent with those of previous research. First, in agreement with Narayanan and Swaminathan's (1996) study..." (C); "which replicates previous simulation results (Wan et al., )" (F). Even sharp criticism stays procedural: "Conceptualizing and measuring selection bias in ratio terms has been continually unsuccessful, to date" (B).

## 9. Tone and Taboos

Register: formal, impersonal, didactic; first person plural only for procedural moves and never for opinion; contractions absent; no rhetorical questions; no exclamation; humor is confined to book reviews (G: "Causality is the cinnamon bun of social science" is quoted from the reviewed book, not the reviewer's own flourish). Out of place in JEM research articles: "we believe" strength claims without hedging (B uses "we submit", markedly rare), superlatives without "may" ("may be the single most effective method", C), first-person singular (only G, a review, uses "I found this to be a very nice book"), informal verbs ("got", "looked at"), and meta-commentary about the reader. Evaluation adjectives stay technical: "attractive alternative", "computationally simple", "conceptually appealing", "realistic", "useful", "meaningful".

## 10. Golden Sentences

Purpose / research question:

1. "Therefore, the purpose of this study is to investigate, through simulations, the relative performance of five item calibration procedures for speeded item responses ... under various testing conditions." (D, purpose statement)
2. "The major purpose of this study was to comparatively evaluate the accuracy of APLAs and cubic spline postsmoothing methods for raw equipercentile equating functions obtained from a range of equating situations featuring different sample sizes and population data." (E, purpose statement)
3. "A secondary question of this study was how APLAs and cubic spline methods compare with 'presmoothing equating' methods based on smoothing the test score distributions before estimating the equipercentile equating function." (E, secondary research question)
4. "This study seeks to compare LR and CDR under conditions of both uniform and nonuniform DIF to provide insight concerning which method is preferred over all forms of DIF observed in applied testing situations." (C, purpose statement)
5. "In this article, we investigate this modeling framework as a potential tool for enhancing an understanding of the causes of DIF in test items." (A, purpose statement)

Gap:

6. "Penfield's (2003a) study did not, however, directly compare LR and CDR under conditions in which the DIF was both uniform and nonuniform, and thus it is still unknown whether CDR provides a relative advantage over LR for both forms of DIF." (C, gap)
7. "Although several approaches have been proposed to account for test speededness, each using a real data set, no further work has been reported comparing these various approaches." (D, gap)
8. "Although a variety of statistical procedures have been developed for detecting DIF, no single method is effective for detecting both uniform and nonuniform DIF." (C, gap)
9. "Once a differential item functioning (DIF) item has been identified, little is known about the examinees for whom the item functions differentially." (A, gap, abstract opening)

Method / design description:

10. "Responses to an artificial test were generated according to a three-parameter logistic model (Lord, 1980)." (C, method opening)
11. "Crossing the two levels each of sample size and ability distribution equality yielded four conditions. Each condition was replicated 200 times." (C, simulation design)
12. "These distributions were chosen to resemble values observed in previous simulation studies." (D, condition justification)
13. "To describe MH, BD, and LR, let us consider a test yielding possible score levels j = 0, 1, ... , J, where Xj denotes the test score associated with the jth score level." (C, model introduction)

Result narration:

14. "All procedures maintained Type I error rates at or below the nominal level of .05 when group ability distributions were equal." (C, result narration)
15. "As the most important observation in this study, the Type I error rates of CDR were consistently below the nominal level of .05 for all conditions (the highest was .045 and the smallest was .03)." (C, headline result)
16. "The values for the two-class mixture models (Models 2 and 4) ranged from 95% to 99%, implying that both models performed well in terms of classifying examinees into the specified groups regardless of various simulation conditions." (D, result + interpretation)
17. "Two patterns hold in most conditions for all three approaches. First, as the test lengthens, bias and IQR become smaller." (F, pattern summary)
18. "Gender was related to class membership (p < .01) although only weakly (rho = .13)." (A, hedged statistical claim)

Hedged claim / interpretation:

19. "This result suggests that CDR may be the single most effective method for simultaneously detecting both uniform and nonuniform DIF in dichotomous items." (C, hedged claim)
20. "But if the IRT model (and its quantitative latent scale) is only a means to estimate CA and CC, the present results suggest that the nonparametric approach may suffice if not excel." (F, hedged claim)

Limitation:

21. "Although the results of this study indicate that CDR was the single most effective method for assessing both uniform and nonuniform DIF, there are several limitations of these results that deserve recognition." (C, limitation pivot)
22. "With that said, the study is limited in several aspects." (F, limitation pivot)
23. "Because of this, generalizations to empirical data should occur with appropriate caution." (F, generalization hedge)

Practical implication:

24. "Since checking the parametric assumptions is a nontrivial task, we recommend using the nonparametric approach as a safer, if not better, method to estimate CA and CC." (F, recommendation)
25. "When one recognizes that DIF items do not universally advantage or disadvantage all members of one group, this practice becomes questionable." (A, implication for practice)
26. "However, because the performance of most smoothing applications can be influenced by several aspects of an equating situation ..., it may be most useful in equating practice to consider both presmoothing and postsmoothing methods rather than to rely exclusively on either approach." (E, balanced recommendation)
